## Lessons

### AI is another platform shift, not the end of computing
> "this is kind of another platform shift and all the new stuff will be built around this for the next 10 or 15 years and then there'll be something else"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=2s)

AI represents a major technological inflection similar to the internet or mobile, not a revolution equivalent to electricity or the industrial revolution. Understanding this framing helps avoid both underestimating and catastrophizing its impact.

### We consistently mispredict how new technologies actually work
> "you can be very very clear that this is the thing and then still be completely unclear how it's going to work"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=4s)

History shows that even when we correctly identify transformative technology, we're often completely wrong about its eventual form and impact. Being certain about the technology but uncertain about outcomes is the normal state.

### Incumbents absorb new tech as features, not transformations
> "when any of these sort of fundamental technology changes the incumbents always try and make it a feature and they try to absorb it"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=7s)

Established companies naturally try to integrate new technologies into their existing business models rather than recognize fundamental shifts. This pattern explains why incumbents often fail to lead in new platform eras.

### Data advantage in LLM training is smaller than assumed
> "everyone's kind of using the same data which is you need such an enormous amount of generalized text that the amount that Google has or that meta has is not actually enough to move to be a kind of fundamental difference"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=12s)

Because LLMs need such enormous amounts of generalized training text, the proprietary data advantages of major tech companies are less decisive than commonly believed. Everyone essentially uses the same available data.

### Google's real threat is resetting consumer search defaults
> "the very high level threat to Google is that you have this moment of discontinuity in which everybody resets their prior and reconsiders their defaults. And so it's no longer just the default that you go and use Google"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=11s)

The danger to Google isn't that other search engines are technically better, but that AI creates a moment where users reconsider their default behavior entirely. Habit switching moments are rare and dangerous for incumbents.

### LLM output quality depends entirely on your use case
> "right or wrong depends, which is a very kind of French philosopher kind of question. The answer is the prompt does it does the prompt have errors? It kind of depends why you wanted it"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=43s)

Whether an AI output is useful or flawed depends completely on context—what you need it for fundamentally changes whether errors matter. This pragmatic view acknowledges that perfect accuracy isn't always necessary.

### Raise your insight baseline using AI as a standard
> "now I can just say is this what chat GPT would have said and if the answer is this is what chat GPT would have said then I didn't publish it"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=45s)

As AI-generated content becomes ubiquitous, the bar for what counts as valuable human insight rises sharply. You should only publish or create things an LLM couldn't already generate. This forces deeper thinking and differentiation.

### Learn how to think, not specific technical skills
> "I think you should be focusing on learning how to think... you'll need to be curious and that you'll have many careers and different kinds of jobs"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=53s)

Education should develop meta-skills like critical thinking, synthesis, and asking good questions rather than training for specific jobs. This adaptability matters more than any particular technical skill, especially as technology changes.

### Pattern recognition develops through exposure to contrasts
> "seeing that contrast and texture and seeing what good looks like, seeing what worked, what didn't work... pattern recognition as much as anything else"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=58s)

Seeing many examples—both exceptional and mediocre—teaches you what quality actually looks like. This calibration only happens through immersion in a domain, not through passive learning.

### Regulate harms, not the technology itself
> "Talking about regulating AI as AI is the wrong level of abstraction. And it's like saying we're going to regulate databases or regulate spreadsheets"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=2NgdQf2GzJg&t=18s)

Broad regulation of AI as a technology category misses the point. Better approach: regulate specific harmful outcomes while maintaining market incentives and innovation. Technology itself is neutral; applications matter.
