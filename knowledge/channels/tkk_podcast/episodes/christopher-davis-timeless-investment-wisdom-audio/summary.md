## Lessons

### Shift perspective through direct relationship
> "no man is a hero to his valet well that that's probably true with kids and grandkids too you know they they have a very narrow way of judging people... switched I realized that you know maybe I was looking at this person with the wrong context"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=183s)

Christopher initially viewed his grandfather as boring and pompous from the family context, but working for him directly revealed his depth, intelligence, and the importance of context in how we judge people. This taught him that narrow viewpoints miss the complexity of others.

### Deep work teaches you more than your audience
> "we write it for ourselves you know the discipline of every week going through you know reporting on any company that had reported earnings what was happening with inflation what was happening with investment returns"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=1440s)

His grandfather explained that writing the weekly insurance letters wasn't about who read them—it was about the discipline of working through data, earnings, inflation, and indices. The process itself was the education, not the external validation.

### Regulation creates business opportunities
> "if I had been happy to charge 50 cents a walk uh that law came into place and I could charge $5 $10 people would pay me to come twice a day uh and you know all of a sudden you know I'm making you know hundred bucks a week"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=610s)

When NYC's pooper scooper law passed, dog walkers suddenly could charge $5-10 instead of 50 cents. Christopher's early entrepreneurial success came from recognizing how a constraint shift creates value—a lesson in seeing regulation as opportunity.

### Avoid status competitions that activate your weaknesses
> "a big part of checking that weakness in me uh is not to get in that pool right and so you know I don't go to Southampton not because I don't think it would be lovely... it's because if I got there I would feel like I want to be here I got to do this"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=7384s)

Christopher recognized his own competitive and envious nature, so he deliberately avoided the Southampton/Aspen/Palm Beach circuit. He chose a modest campground in Maine instead—not due to deprivation, but to design his environment to avoid triggering compulsive comparisons.

### Model the life you want, don't preach it
> "you get into a lot of trouble when you want your kids to live a different lifestyle than you're living yes uh when you're getting a chauffeur to drive you to work and you're telling your 15 or 16 year-old to go get a job at McDonald's uh he said that's going to create a lot of resentment"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=7871s)

Charlie Munger told him that trying to give your kids a different lifestyle than you live creates resentment. Kids imitate what they see, not what you tell them. Authenticity in how you live is more powerful than rules.

### Teach through narrative and biography, not formulas
> "Oppenheimer will probably draw more people into physics uh people into physics uh uh you know than stem funding in high schools will... we're wired to sort of have Heroes and to admire them you know the whole story Homer everything"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=960s)

His father turned abstract business concepts into stories—about hippies in sandals trying to start Apple, about markets and Intel. Humans are wired to learn through story and hero-imitation, not formulas. Science education should include the biography of discovery.

### Meaningful engagement trumps financial minimization
> "if you really think the goal goal of work is to invest as little as you can in order to get as much payment as you can of course it's going to suck... We crave competence... we're tinkerers you know we we want to improve we want to learn"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=1560s)

He observed college students obsessed with remote work to minimize effort, then felt alienated and depressed. Humans crave competence, learning, relationships, and contribution—not just payment. Work done with engagement creates identity and pride.

### Trust and candor require small groups and dinners
> "they had a tradition where the night before the board meeting there was a dinner the board meeting there was a dinner the dinner was held at the CEO's house and nobody else is invited just the the the directors... you develop a deep culture of trust"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=8700s)

The Washington Post board tradition of hosting a dinner the night before board meetings—just directors, no one else—created deep trust, candor, and accountability that made board governance effective. Size matters; intimacy enables honesty.

### Order life by concentric circles of meaning
> "these sort of concentric circles and you know in some ways the Outer Circle you think it's the biggest it matters the most I have a feeling the end it may matter the least but you still want to get it right"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=9396s)

When facing death, an executive realized the outer circles (the firm) matter less than inner circles (family, self). Success isn't measured by scope but by depth—securing what matters most in the narrowest circles at life's end.

### Gratitude for good fortune is a daily practice
> "there's a Psalm uh that begins uh behold uh uh the day the the Lord has made rejoice and be glad in it... I don't want in that moment to regret that I didn't appreciate just how lucky I had it today the day before all that changed"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=o6rgXYt7BnE&t=8736s)

He starts each day with the Psalm: 'Behold the day the Lord has made, rejoice and be glad in it.' Not because life is perfect, but because luck is fragile and tomorrow could change. Daily appreciation prevents regret.
