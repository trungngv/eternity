# The Man Who Said No to Rupert Murdoch: Barry Diller on Power, Media, and Getting Rich at 80
**Source:** [Watch on YouTube](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ)
**Date:** --

## Lessons

### Failure teaches far more than success ever can
> "Success teaches you nothing, but failure sure teaches you a lot."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=0s)

Success creates complacency and teaches little about improvement. Failure, by contrast, forces reflection and provides invaluable lessons for future decisions. This principle underpins Barry's iterative approach to building and decision-making throughout his career.

### Lack of confidence can paradoxically drive excellence
> "I never had a sense of self because I didn't think I deserved a sense of self and so I never could claim that. But that lack of a sense of self enabled me in the earliest part of my career to please others to such an extent that it got me going."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=360s)

Not having a strong sense of self, while seemingly a weakness, enabled Barry to focus entirely on pleasing others and earning their confidence. This absence of ego actually propelled his early career forward more effectively than innate confidence might have.

### Be fully present in your current role, not your next one
> "If you get lucky enough to get a position that you don't have experience for, that you haven't mastered, and then you just go about doing it. The sparks you set off naturally impress others and lead. They drag you to the next step."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=660s)

Many people treat jobs as stepping stones toward their real ambition, never fully committing to their current position. The most valuable work comes from complete presence and excellence in what you're doing right now, which naturally leads to advancement.

### Protect your instincts from the corrosion of cynicism
> "The most important thing and the thing that I have been reminded of continuously is to keep scrubbing my instincts clean. Being naive, not being cynical, being able to not let life experience infect your basic instinct, your ability to know a good idea from a bad idea."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=1500s)

As you gain experience, the natural tendency is to become cynical and sophisticated. Maintaining the ability to recognize good ideas from bad ones requires deliberately fighting against this corrosion and holding onto naiveté, which is an ongoing discipline.

### Embrace iteration through mistakes as your primary strategy
> "Almost everything in my life iteration one dumb step in front of the other course correcting as you go is the only process I'm any good at."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=2520s)

Building something new requires taking dumb steps forward, hitting walls, and course-correcting continuously. This iterative process of bouncing off obstacles is the only viable way to navigate truly unknown territory and build unprecedented things.

### Vigorous confrontation surfaces better truths than consensus
> "I love confrontation because in confrontation out comes stuff that if you're listening, you're listening, no matter how noisy that confrontation gets, if your ear is open, you're listening and you'll hear something."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=2400s)

Heated debate and direct disagreement, while uncomfortable for many, creates an environment where ideas are tested rigorously. The noise of passionate argument reveals genuine insights better than polite consensus ever can.

### Your responsibility clock starts the moment you know
> "The second you know it, the clock is ticking and you have to have that in your brain. Every second after that, what you do about that is now your absolute responsibility."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=3240s)

Once you become aware of a problem—whether theft, misconduct, or product failure—the clock of your responsibility begins immediately. Every action you take after that moment becomes your responsibility; procrastination or inaction are choices you own.

### Instinct beats data-driven decisions for creative work
> "Numbers can tell you what has happened in the past but they won't tell you what's happened in the future... Making instinctive decisions is living with great insecurity. It's a delusion."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=1320s)

While data can show what happened in the past, it cannot predict creative success. Relying heavily on data provides false comfort; truly valuable creative decisions require trusting instinct and accepting the inherent insecurity that comes with it.

### Independence requires accepting the binary choice squarely
> "Either you are or you're not. It's binary... I couldn't accept that I was not. And that's what got me out."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=2820s)

The decision to build something of your own versus remaining a successful executive is fundamentally binary—you either are or you're not. This isn't a spectrum; avoiding this confrontation keeps you trapped between two worlds without committing to either.

### Curiosity is the ultimate measure of success
> "Success for me is remaining curious. The only thing that drives me is my curiosity and if I'm curious then that is success."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=zyw1HVOqpCQ&t=3480s)

Beyond wealth, power, or achievement, sustained curiosity—the drive to keep learning and exploring—represents true success. When curiosity remains alive, you stay engaged with life and work in a meaningful way.
