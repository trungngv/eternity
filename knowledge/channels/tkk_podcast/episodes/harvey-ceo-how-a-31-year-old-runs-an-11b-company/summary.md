## Lessons

### Daily task reranking reveals what truly matters
> "every day I rank everything I need to do. And so it's daily and then I refresh it and then I cross them out. And the reranking is I think really important because what I found is the more times I click into that doc that's called the list and I reank something the more I think about what I'm doing like meta about my thoughts."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=120s)

The act of reranking tasks daily forces metacognitive reflection about priorities. This practice of repeatedly reviewing and reordering your list helps you think more clearly about what actually deserves your time, making it the single best predictor of execution quality.

### Writing justifications filters out unnecessary commitments
> "When I get really bad at it, I ask my chief of staff to force me to write a paragraph about why I'm going to take a meeting. And it's so easy if you do it that way because for 99% of meetings or events or whatever, you start writing the first sentence and you're like, I don't want to do this."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=180s)

Requiring yourself to write a full paragraph justifying a commitment reveals whether it truly aligns with your priorities. This simple friction mechanism naturally filters out 99% of distractions without relying on willpower, since most commitments fail the written test.

### Build organizational systems before fixing bottlenecks
> "Like if there is a part of the company that is running super well and there are no bottlenecks. I ignore it entirely. Like absolutely entirely. And I try to only focus on the things that are like burning."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=240s)

Effective founders balance two skills: building scalable systems/processes and identifying critical bottlenecks. You must do both—building without fixing bottlenecks wastes effort, but fixing bottlenecks in chaotic organizations doesn't compound. Completely ignore what's working well to concentrate on what's burning.

### Choose hard long-term wins over easy short-term looks
> "what people want to do is they want to start making improvements so that third party can see it immediately. So they'll start doing the meetings because every time you do that meeting, you get a pat on the back from your VC. But deep down you're like, 'The reason we have a revenue problem is because there's a problem with product.'"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=360s)

People naturally pursue activities that produce immediate, visible progress to others, even when they don't address root causes. Truly effective leaders ignore external validation and work on the real problem even if it takes months to show results and appears to be failure along the way.

### Use reversibility and P0 priority to filter decisions
> "determine if something's a one-way or two-way door immediately, like that's the first thing, right? Um, with the idea that baseline 99.9% of things is a two-way door. The second thing is what is go back to what is your number one priority right now? And based off of what your P0 is, does this help the P0? Does this hurt the P0 or is this irrelevant to the P0?"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=420s)

Most decisions (99.9%) are reversible two-way doors. Filter decisions by: (1) Reversibility—can you undo it? (2) P0 impact—does it help your top priority? If irrelevant, decide fast. If harmful, say no immediately. (3) Realistic ownership—who actually does the work? This framework scales decision-making across teams.

### Hire for resilience and learning velocity, not pedigree
> "No one at the company ever has been let go because they made too many mistakes. It's never happened. They always broke... the thing that broke was they started to get decision paralysis. They started to not be able to scale... are you able like what is your rate of learning? Can you have failures? Can you learn from them? What is your resilience?"
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=1920s)

The most critical hiring criterion is resilience—the ability to make mistakes, learn rapidly, and keep moving. People fail at scale not from making errors but from decision paralysis and inability to delegate. Founders often over-weight credentials while under-weight adaptability and growth rate.

### Face difficult decisions early to build stress immunity
> "if there are 10 things that you're really stressed about, right, you should probably try to do those early on in the company because if you get really good at them and you every time you do one of those things, it should reduce your stress even if you do a bad job."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=1440s)

Deliberately tackle stressful decisions (firing, hard conversations, taking calculated risks) when the stakes are relatively low. This builds an immunity that lets you handle them easily at scale. Waiting until you're large means a single bad decision-maker can destroy the company.

### Make fast decisions, learn through rapid iteration cycles
> "everything that I regret at the company or whenever I regret something, it's not making a decision fast enough. It's not I made the wrong decision. What I've always regretted is sitting at the bottom of the stairs for too long instead of jump up a stair and then I'll figure it out and then jump up a stair."
[Watch this moment →](https://youtube.com/watch?v=j6jgpb8kCR0&t=1560s)

Indecision is the only real failure; wrong decisions are just data. Fast decision-making compresses feedback loops, letting you course-correct quickly. Founders regret delays, not errors. Speed gives you the option value of learning and adapting faster than competitors can even plan.
